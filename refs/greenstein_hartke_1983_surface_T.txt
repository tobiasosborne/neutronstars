                                                                                     ABSTRACT
                                          The magnetic poles of a neutron star are expected to be hotter than the remainder of its
                                        surface. The observed X-ray intensity of blackbody radiation will then be modulated as rotation
                                        carries each hot spot across the visible disk of the star. Two models for the distribution of
                                        temperature over the stellar surface are presented, and the predicted “light” curves and spectra
                                        synthesized.
                                          We then interpret the X-ray emission from two radio pulsars as examples of this effect. Both
                                        the light curve and flux from PSR 1509-58 are well described by a model in which the polar
                                        caps are strongly heated by the magnetosphere. The light curve of PSR 1055 - 52, if blackbody
                                        in origin, implies that it is nearly aligned, a conclusion of particular interest in that this source
                                        shows interpulses.
                                        Subject headings: pulsars — radiation mechanisms — stars: neutron — X-rays: sources

                                                                            I. INTRODUCTION
                            X-ray observations with the Einstein Observatory (HEAO 2) are capable of detecting thermal radiation from
                          neutron stars if their temperatures exceed several times 105 K in the best cases (Helfand, Chanan, and Novick
                          1980). A number of point sources coincident with radio pulsars have been found, some of which may be blackbody
                          in spectrum. Here we analyze a feature of this emission which has not been sufficiently discussed, viz., that it
                          should be pulsed. The magnetic poles of a neutron star are expected to be significantly hotter than the remainder
                          of its surface. The observed X-ray intensity will be modulated as rotation carries each hot spot across the visible
                          disk of the star (Smoluchowski 1972). Because the emission is blackbody in nature, each pole radiates isotropically
                          rather than into a narrow cone: the signature of the process is a broad pulse filling a significant fraction of the
                          pulsar duty cycle, as opposed to the more narrow pulses characteristic of the radio emission.
                             In § II we roughly analyze the expected distribution of temperature over the surface of a neutron star, and in
                          § III the “light” curve and spectrum of the source are described. Section IV interprets the X-ray emission from
                          the recently discovered pulsar PSR 1509 — 58 as an example of this phenomenon, and uses the absence of modulation
                          in the X-ray emission from PSR 1055 — 52 to constrain the geometry of the source.

                                                                  II. DISTRIBUTION OF TEMPERATURE OVER THE SURFACE OF THE STAR
                                                                                                      a) Polar Cap Heating
                            Within a wide variety of models a significant flux of energy is deposited onto the polar cap from the pulsar
                          magnetosphere. Estimates of polar temperatures expected from this process (Cheng and Ruderman 1980; Helfand,
                          Chanan, and Novick 1980; Arons 1981) predict fluxes potentially observable with the Einstein observatory and
                          temperatures greater than 106 K.
                            We expect such high temperatures to be confined very closely to the polar cap itself. The thermal conductivity of
                          neutron star matter increases with density, increasing roughly as p2 near the surface in the calculations of Flowers
                          and Itoh (1976: see their Fig. 8). The density itself increases inward extremely rapidly. Very close to the polar cap
                          heat diffuses outward in all directions. However, once it has propagated even a matter of meters, it encounters
                          matter of high thermal conductivity below the surface, which effectively channels its flow downward and limits any
                          further horizontal spread. Furthermore, the transport of energy perpendicular to magnetic field lines, i.e., horizontally
                          at the polar cap, is a far slower process than transport parallel to the field (Canuto and Chiuderi 1970; Itoh
                          1975). We therefore expect the high-temperature region to extend only slightly beyond the polar cap itself.

                                                                                 IV. TWO PULSARS
                                                      a) PSR 1055 — 52: The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Nighttime
                             In the accompanying paper (Cheng and Helfand 1983) the detection of X-rays from PSR 1055 — 52 is reported,
                          and the possibility that the emission is blackbody discussed. Although a synchrotron spectrum best fits the data,
                          a blackbody form cannot be ruled out at present. If the source is blackbody, the observed absence of modulation
                          in this source has several important consequences.
                             Cheng and Helfand (1983) report an upper limit on the modulation M of 3%. This can only be understood if
                          PSR 1055 — 52 is a nearly aligned rotator. In the context of the hot-spot model, equation (23) implies 'F < 7° if
                          6 = 'F. Relaxing the restriction 0 = 'F softens this conclusion, but only slightly: if a maximum offset of 10° between
                          the line of sight and the magnetic pole is allowed, ¥ can be increased to 14°. From Figure 8 a similar conclusion
                          obtains within the context of the model of anisotropic heating from below. We have also explored the consequences
                          of relaxing the assumption         ^ Xy and find that a 3% modulation implies KJK^ ä 0.9 (for 0 = lF). We regard
                          such large values of        to be physically impossible.
                             This result is difficult to understand if pulsar magnetic axes are randomly inclined relative to their rotation axis.
                          If so, the probability that a pole lies within an angle ¥ of the spin axis is 1 — cos 'F, which is 1% for ¥ = 7° and
                          3% for 'F = 14°. This cannot be ascribed to any alignment mechanism, for, with its period P = 0.197 s and
                          spin-down age of 5 x 105 yr, PSR 1055 — 52 is relatively young. We note in this connection that Narayan and
                          Vivekanand (1982a) have reached a somewhat similar conclusion in an analysis of the polarization swings of pulsars:
                          they find a statistically significant preponderance of small values of 0 which cannot be ascribed to alignment.

                                                                              b) PSR 1509-58
                             PSR 1509-58 is unique in having been discovered as an X-ray pulsar before it was detected as a radio pulsar

                             We conclude that a model for PSR 1509-58 can be developed in which the X-ray component of the emission is
                          blackbody in nature. Within this model magnetospheric heating of the polar regions is very strong, and there is some
                          indication that it extends over a significantly larger area than a classical polar cap. We wish to point out that it is
                          somewhat surprising that a simple model based on a purely dipolar configuration succeeds at all. The dynamical
                          implosion in which a neutron star is formed is turbulent in nature, and the stellar field, whatever its initial form,
                          is scrambled in the collapse. Upon formation, the pulsar magnetic field could not remotely approach a dipole
                          configuration. Observations of X-ray light curves map the surface field, as opposed to inferences drawn from
                          behavior far from the star (e.g., at the speed-of-light cylinder) at which the higher moments have dropped off.
                          The observation of PSR 1509-58 is unique in this regard, and it may imply that magnetic field decay has acted
                          to smooth out all but the dipole component.
                             It may be significant that this source has the highest period derivative of any known pulsar. Furthermore,
                           PSR 0655 + 64, the pulsar with the smallest known period derivative, has one of the lowest limits on its X-ray
                           luminosity (Helfand, unpublished). The implication is that the stronger the torque, the more intense the backflow
                           of energy from the magnetosphere onto the star, and the stronger the X-ray emission from the polar cap.
                              Finally, we note that if our interpretation is correct, Seward and Harnden (1982) have identified the smallest
                           known object at stellar distances. The radiating surface is one-half mile in extent and is ~ 14,000 It-yr away.
